# Why your nose runs in the morning, Grandma

Here's what's going on, in plain words.

**While you sleep, your nose is on "night shift."** At night, your body switches into rest-and-repair mode. Part of that mode tells the little glands inside your nose to make extra mucus — it's how your nose keeps itself moist and catches dust while you breathe for seven or eight hours straight.

**Lying down lets it pool.** When you're flat on your back, gravity isn't pulling that mucus down your throat the way it does when you're upright. So it just… sits there. Your nasal tissues also get a bit more blood flow when you're horizontal, which makes them puffy and full — like a sponge that's been soaking all night.

**Then you wake up, and three things hit at once.** You sit up (gravity suddenly pulls everything downward), the bedroom air is cold and dry (especially with the heat running), and your body shifts from night-mode to day-mode. Your nose has a built-in reflex that says "cold dry air? — make more mucus to protect yourself!"

**Here's the part that's specifically about being a little older.** When we're young, the nose has a kind of dimmer switch that turns the mucus on gently and off again quickly. As we age, that dimmer switch gets a bit sticky — it overshoots. So instead of a small, brief response, you get a bigger one that takes longer to settle down. Doctors actually have a name for this: **"senile rhinitis."** It's very common and completely benign — it just means the nose's thermostat isn't as precise as it used to be.

**So the runny nose is really three things stacking up:** a night's worth of pooled mucus, plus a cold-air reflex when you stand up, plus an older nose that responds a little too enthusiastically. Within ten or fifteen minutes — once you've warmed up, moved around, and had something warm to drink — everything calms down and you're fine for the day.

**If it bothers you**, a doctor can prescribe a nasal spray called **ipratropium** that quiets the over-eager glands. A humidifier in the bedroom helps too. But honestly — it's not a sign anything's wrong. It's just a normal feature of an experienced nose.
